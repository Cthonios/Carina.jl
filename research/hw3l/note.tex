  \normalsize Research note}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A tetrahedral element for very large deformation and deviatoric plasticity,
aimed at removing the stabilization that the composite-tetrahedron and
variational-multiscale lineages both carry. Three fields --- motion, volumetric
strain, and pressure --- are coupled through a Hu--Washizu functional. The
deviatoric response is evaluated pointwise at quadrature, so the constitutive
law is an unmodified small-strain algorithm, and both auxiliary fields are
discontinuous, so their elimination is element-local. The material is not
arbitrary, and \S\ref{sec:matreq} says how far it is not: the stored energy
must split exactly into volumetric and isochoric parts, with the volumetric
part quadratic in the variable that gets projected. Five of the seven materials
implemented in Norma admit the split and two admit it quadratically --- Hencky
in $\log J$, and the Simo--Hughes $J_2$ model in $J-1$ --- so the material and
its volumetric variable are not chosen independently.

The working hypothesis is that volumetric locking and spurious soft modes are
two failure directions of a single inf-sup condition, so that an inf-sup stable
displacement--pressure pair removes both without a penalty, a subgrid model, or
a tuned coefficient. With both auxiliary fields in one space the functional
reduces exactly to mean dilatation (\S\ref{sec:meandilatation}): the mechanism
is classical, and the open question was which pressure space to project onto.

That question is answered here. Of the four element-local pairs measured, three
are unstable --- constant and linear discontinuous pressure over a quadratic
tetrahedron, and the linear one enriched by an interior bubble --- while the
full three-dimensional Crouzeix--Raviart pair, with face bubbles as well,
settles on a discrete inf-sup constant of $0.2968$, above the $0.2216$ of the
Taylor--Hood control that validates the test. The face bubbles are what
distinguishes the two enrichments, and they are shared between neighbors rather
than element-local, so the stable pair costs global degrees of freedom that an
earlier version of this note claimed it did not: $3.5$ times the displacement
dimension of $P_2/P_0$ on the same mesh. A degree-of-freedom count that clears
a pair remains necessary and not sufficient --- two of the three failures clear
it. The soft-mode argument against a constant pressure did not survive assembly
and has been withdrawn; the locking argument did, and is the case for
enrichment. The measurement this formulation is intended to address --- whether
plastic strain fields are smooth --- remains untested, and will be made on the
Simo--Hughes model both codes ship rather than on the logarithmic return map
specified here.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem statement}

Tetrahedra are the only practical choice for meshing complex geometry
automatically. Their performance in the regimes that matter here has been the
obstacle: near-incompressible response at bulk-to-shear ratios
$\kappa/\mu \lesssim 10^4$, isochoric plasticity, large rotation, and weak
shocks in stiff-bulk materials. The target is one spatial formulation covering
all four, with the time integrator the only thing that changes between
quasi-static and dynamic regimes.

Two established remedies fall short in ways that are instructive, and the
diagnosis below is the reason for the choices that follow.

\paragraph{Constant pressure relieves locking at the cost of soft modes.}
The composite tetrahedron \citep{Thoutireddy.etal:2002, Ostien.etal:2016} and
its five-field extension \citep{Foulk.etal:2021} mitigate volumetric locking
with an element-constant pressure. \citet{Foulk.etal:2021} report that this
choice --- and it is the choice, not the element --- produces a deleterious
soft mode ``common to both the quadratic and composite tetrahedral element with
constant pressure formulations,'' remedied there by a convex energy penalty.
The mechanism is direct: an element-constant multiplier cannot see
near-isochoric displacement patterns that vary within the element. Those
patterns cost only shear energy, so at $\kappa/\mu \gg 1$ they are soft, and
confined deformation excites them.

\paragraph{Residual-based stabilization contaminates the deviator.}
Variational-multiscale stabilization of linear tetrahedra
\citep{Scovazzi:2015} introduces a subgrid pressure of the form
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:vms}
  p' = c_p \, \tau \left( \kappa \Div \dot{\disp} - \dot{p} \right),
  \qquad
  \tau = c_\tau \, \Delta t,
\end{equation}
which is added to the stress before the constitutive update sees it. Two
consequences are structural rather than incidental. The spatial solution
inherits a dependence on the \emph{time step} through $\tau$, so there is no
well-defined quasi-static limit --- $\tau$ is whatever $\Delta t$ happens to
be. And a tuned coefficient $c_\tau$ modulates a term that reaches the
deviator, and therefore the plastic return map, which is the observed origin of
oscillations in equivalent plastic strain. No choice of $c_\tau$ removes this,
because the contamination path is the formulation.

\paragraph{The common cause.} Both are symptoms of one condition. The
volumetric constraint space must be \emph{rich enough} to see the
near-isochoric modes, or soft modes survive; and \emph{poor enough} not to
over-constrain the displacement space, or the element locks. That is the
inf-sup condition, read in both directions. Neither difficulty is a
stabilization problem. Both are consequences of a pair that sits outside the
admissible window, and the remedy is to choose a pair inside it.

\section{Continuum formulation}

\subsection{Kinematics and the volumetric--isochoric split}
\label{sec:kinematics}

A body $B \subset \Reals^3$ undergoes a motion $\bx = \disp(\bX, t)$ with
deformation gradient $\F := \Grad\disp$ and Jacobian $J := \det\F > 0$. The
multiplicative factorization
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:isosplit}
  \F = J^{1/3}\bar{\F},
  \qquad
  \bar{\F} := J^{-1/3}\F,
  \qquad
  \det\bar{\F} = 1,
\end{equation}
separates the two kinematic contributions this formulation treats differently:
$J$ carries everything the mixed treatment exists for, and $\bar{\F}$ carries
everything the plasticity acts on.

The mixed field is a scalar function of $J$ alone. Let
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:thetadef}
  \theta = \theta(J),
  \qquad \theta'(J) > 0,
  \qquad \theta(1) = 0,
  \qquad \theta'(1) = 1,
\end{equation}
be a strictly increasing volumetric strain measure, normalized to vanish with
unit slope in the undeformed state. The normalization has two consequences. It
makes $\kappa$ in \eqref{eq:energy} the physical bulk modulus for every
admissible $\theta$, so that two instances of the formulation are calibrated to
the same material rather than to two different ones. And since
$\partial J/\partial\F = J\F^{-\mathsf T}$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dtheta}
  \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\F} = \theta'(J)\, J\, \F^{-\mathsf T},
\end{equation}
so \eqref{eq:thetadef} also forces $\partial\theta/\partial\F = \Id$ at
$\F = \Id$ --- which is what makes the mixed pairing reduce to the Stokes
pairing independently of the choice of $\theta$ (\S\ref{sec:fe-spaces}).

Two choices are used below and are developed in \S\ref{sec:instances}: the
logarithmic measure $\theta = \log J$, and $\theta = J - 1$. Both satisfy
\eqref{eq:thetadef}, and by Remark~\ref{rem:thetachoice} they nevertheless give
different elements.

\subsection{Stored energy}
\label{sec:energy}

The formulation is developed for a stored energy that splits exactly along
\eqref{eq:isosplit}, with a volumetric part quadratic in $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:energy}
  W(\F) = W\supvol(\theta) + W\supiso(\bar{\F}),
  \qquad
  W\supvol(\theta) = \tfrac{\kappa}{2}\theta^2 .
\end{equation}
Both halves of \eqref{eq:energy} are requirements on the material rather than
conventions, they are requirements of different strength, and
\S\ref{sec:matreq} separates them and reports which materials meet them.

Given \eqref{eq:energy}, the volumetric constitutive relation is linear,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:constitutive}
  p = \partial W\supvol/\partial\theta = \kappa\theta ,
\end{equation}
and it is this linearity --- not the choice of $\theta$, and not the
logarithmic measure specifically --- that the element-local elimination of
\S\ref{sec:local} rests on.

The isochoric response is whatever $W\supiso$ the material supplies. It is
evaluated at the pointwise $\bar{\F}$ and is never projected, which is the
property \S\ref{sec:plasticity} requires. Plasticity acts entirely inside
$W\supiso$, through an isochoric internal variable evolved by a return map;
neither \eqref{eq:energy} nor \eqref{eq:constitutive} changes when it does.

\subsection{Three-field functional}
\label{sec:hw}

Only the volumetric response is treated mixedly. Introduce an independent
volumetric strain $\bar\theta$ and constrain it to $\theta(\disp)$ through a
multiplier $\bar p$, which at stationarity is the pressure:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:hw}
  \begin{split}
    \Pi[\disp, \bar\theta, \bar p] & :=
    \int_B W\supvol(\bar\theta) \diff V
    + \int_B W\supdev\!\left(\edev(\disp)\right) \diff V
    + \int_B \bar p \left(\theta(\disp) - \bar\theta\right) \diff V
    \\
    & \quad
    - \int_B R\, \bB \cdot \disp \diff V
    - \int_{\partial_T B} \bT \cdot \disp \diff S .
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
The deviatoric energy is evaluated at the \emph{pointwise} strain
$\edev(\disp)$ and carries no auxiliary field. This is deliberate and is
discussed in Remark~\ref{rem:whynodev}.

\subsection{What the functional reduces to}
\label{sec:meandilatation}

Equation \eqref{eq:hw} belongs to an established lineage, and identifying that
lineage is a precondition for assessing what in the formulation is new.

Put $\bar\theta$ and $\bar p$ in the same discrete space $Q_h$, which
\S\ref{sec:fe-spaces} does for reasons of its own. Stationarity in $\bar\theta$
then gives $\bar p = \kappa\bar\theta$ \emph{exactly} rather than after a
projection, and stationarity in $\bar p$ gives $\bar\theta = \Pi_h\,\theta$,
the $L^2$ projection of $\theta$ onto $Q_h$. The constraint term of
\eqref{eq:hw} then vanishes identically:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:constraint-vanishes}
  \int_B \bar p \left(\theta - \bar\theta\right) \diff V
  = \kappa \int_B \bar\theta \left(\theta - \Pi_h \theta\right) \diff V = 0,
\end{equation}
because $\kappa\bar\theta \in Q_h$ while $(\theta - \Pi_h\theta) \perp Q_h$ by
the definition of the projection. What is left is a functional in the motion
alone,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:collapsed}
  \Pi[\disp] = \int_B W\supiso\!\left(\bar{\F}(\disp)\right) \diff V
             + \int_B \tfrac{\kappa}{2}\left(\Pi_h \theta\right)^2 \diff V,
\end{equation}
whose stationarity conditions are exactly the three-field equations of
\S\ref{sec:variations}.

\begin{remark}[This is mean dilatation, and the pressure space is the contribution]
\label{rem:meandilatation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:collapsed} is a displacement formulation carrying a
projected volumetric strain --- the mean-dilatation construction of
\citet{SimoTaylorPister:1985}, and the mechanism of the $\bar{B}$ and
$\bar{F}$ families. Three consequences follow.

The reduction is \emph{exact}, not an approximation, and the reason is
\eqref{eq:constraint-vanishes} and nothing else: $\bar p$ lies in $Q_h$, and
$(\theta - \Pi_h\theta)$ is orthogonal to $Q_h$ by the definition of the
projection. No property of the logarithmic measure is used, and none of a
quadratic $W\supvol$. An earlier version of this note asserted that the
exactness followed from the Hencky measure; that was wrong, and the error
mattered, because it made a property of the \emph{discretization} --- that the
two auxiliary fields share one space --- look like a property of the
\emph{material}. What the material must actually supply is weaker, and is set
out in \S\ref{sec:matreq}: an exact volumetric--isochoric split, without which
the subtraction leaves volumetric content inside the ``deviatoric'' term and
the formulation is incorrect with no accompanying symptom. The implementation
in Norma refuses materials that fail it.

The novelty here is therefore \emph{not the mechanism}. Projecting the
volumetric strain is four decades old. What is open, and what
\S\ref{sec:fe-spaces} and \S\ref{sec:results} are about, is which space to
project onto --- a question the classical elements answer by defaulting to a
single constant per element, and which \S\ref{sec:beta} shows is not a free
choice.

And it means this formulation shares its mechanism with the $\bar{F}$ lineage
it is meant to improve on. The claim is not that the mechanism is new; it is
that
the mechanism was never the problem, and that the constant-pressure choice made
alongside it was. Whether replacing that choice is enough is what the
verification list of \S\ref{sec:verify} is for, and item~4 --- the smoothness
of plastic strain fields, the failure this formulation is intended to
address --- remains untested.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What the formulation requires of the material}
\label{sec:matreq}

Section~\ref{sec:meandilatation} shows the reduction is exact whenever $\bar p$
and $\bar\theta$ share a space. That is a statement about the discretization
and says nothing about the material. What the material must supply is
\eqref{eq:energy}, and the two halves of it are requirements of quite different
strength. Separating them matters, because an earlier version of this note ran
them together with a third condition that is not a requirement of this section
at all.

\paragraph{An exact volumetric--isochoric split.} This condition is
structural. The
element evaluates $W$ at the pointwise deformation, subtracts the pointwise
volumetric energy and adds it back at the projected strain, so the subtraction
must leave no volumetric remainder:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:matsplit}
  W(\F) = W\supvol(J) + W\supiso(\bar{\F}),
  \qquad
  \bar{\F} := J^{-1/3}\F,
  \qquad
  \det\bar{\F} = 1 .
\end{equation}
Whether a material satisfies \eqref{eq:matsplit} is decidable without knowing
$W\supvol$: if the split exists then $W(\F) - W(\bar{\F}) = W\supvol(J) -
W\supvol(1)$ depends on $J$ alone, so holding $J$ fixed while varying the
isochoric part must leave the difference unmoved. Column~2 of
Table~\ref{tab:split} is that test, run over every material Norma implements.
Five of the seven satisfy it, four more than the implementation currently
admits.

\paragraph{A quadratic $W\supvol$.} This condition is a convenience and not a
necessity. Stationarity in $\bar\theta$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pbar-general}
  \bar p = \Pi_h\!\left(\partial W\supvol/\partial\bar\theta\right)
\end{equation}
in general. When $W\supvol$ is quadratic its derivative is linear,
$\kappa\bar\theta$ already lies in $Q_h$, the projection in
\eqref{eq:pbar-general} is the identity, and $\bar p = \kappa\bar\theta$ holds
\emph{pointwise}. That is what makes the elimination of \S\ref{sec:local} a
single linear element-local solve rather than a local Newton iteration on
$\bar\theta$. The reduction \eqref{eq:collapsed} is exact either way; only the
cost of the elimination changes.

\paragraph{The logarithmic measure itself.} This condition is required by
nothing in
the present section. It is required by \S\ref{sec:plasticity}, for an
unrelated reason: only in logarithmic strain is the constitutive law
algebraically linear, which is what allows a small-strain return map to be
reused verbatim.

\paragraph{The restriction taken here.} The formulation is developed for
materials satisfying \eqref{eq:matsplit} \emph{with} a quadratic $W\supvol$.
Table~\ref{tab:split} admits exactly two, and they do not agree on the
variable: Hencky is quadratic in $\log J$ to $4.4\times10^{-15}$, and the
Simo--Hughes $J_2$ model that Norma and Carina both ship is quadratic in $J-1$
to $1.1\times10^{-14}$. Each is exact in its own variable and wrong by $17\%$
in the other, so material and volumetric variable are not independently
selectable, and neither choice generalizes to the other material.
\S\ref{sec:instances} develops both.

The three remaining split materials --- neo-Hookean, its reciprocal, and
Seth--Hill --- are excluded here. The formulation does not fail on them; the
cost of admitting them exceeds the present benefit, and the requirements are
set out in Remark~\ref{rem:nonquadratic}.

\begin{remark}[Extending to a non-quadratic $W\supvol$]
\label{rem:nonquadratic}
Nothing in \S\ref{sec:hw} or \S\ref{sec:meandilatation} breaks. Three things
change, all in the implementation.

First, \eqref{eq:projection} stops being a linear solve. $\bar\theta = \Pi_h
\theta$ still comes from one mass-matrix solve, but $\bar p$ must then be
obtained from \eqref{eq:pbar-general}, which for a nonlinear $W\supvol{}'$
requires projecting a nonlinear function of $\bar\theta$ --- a local Newton
iteration per element, on as many scalars as $Q_h$ has dimensions per element.
The cost per iteration is small and the solve is element-local, so
\S\ref{sec:local} survives,
but it introduces a local solve that can fail to converge where the global one
would not.

Second, the tangent acquires a term in $W\supvol{}''(\bar\theta)$ that is no
longer constant, so the factorization reused across every Newton iteration in
\S\ref{sec:local} must be refreshed whenever $\bar\theta$ moves.

Third, the choice of volumetric variable is no longer determined. For a quadratic $W\supvol$ the variable is whichever one makes it
quadratic, and Table~\ref{tab:split} settles it. For a non-quadratic
$W\supvol$ any monotone $\theta(J)$ is admissible, and by
Remark~\ref{rem:thetachoice} the choice changes the element. It would have to
be justified rather than read off.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The volumetric variable is a modeling choice]
\label{rem:thetachoice}
Projecting $\log J$ and projecting $J$ do not give the same element. Measured
on one \code{TETRA10} under a non-affine displacement, with a Hencky material
so that both variants use the same exact $W\supvol$, the element energies
differ by
$3.9\times10^{-4}$ at $0.5\%$ strain,
$3.8\times10^{-3}$ at $5\%$,
$1.3\times10^{-2}$ at $20\%$ and
$2.8\times10^{-2}$ at $50\%$.
The difference is $O(\text{strain})$ and vanishes with it, so both are
consistent discretizations of the same continuum problem; they are simply
different elements, and they differ by about $3\%$ in exactly the
finite-deformation regime this formulation exists to address. Which variable is
projected is therefore a modeling decision, not a change of notation.
\end{remark}

\label{sec:instances}

\S\ref{sec:kinematics}--\S\ref{sec:meandilatation} requires and differ in every
instance-specific quantity the formulation touches; Table~\ref{tab:instances}
collects both columns.

The Hencky instance is the one \eqref{eq:hencky} defines: with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:hencky}
  \eps := \tfrac{1}{2}\log\C \in \Syms,
  \qquad \C := \transpose{\F}\F,
\end{equation}
evaluated by inverse scaling-and-squaring with Pad\'e approximants, the
volumetric--deviatoric decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:split}
  \eps = \tfrac{1}{3}\theta\Id + \edev,
  \qquad \theta = \trace\eps = \log J,
  \qquad \edev = \dev\eps,
\end{equation}
is orthogonal in the Frobenius inner product, and the split of
\eqref{eq:energy} is inherited from the strain measure rather than imposed on
it. The Simo--Hughes instance imposes it instead, through the $J^{-2/3}$ factor
in $\bar{~b}^{\mathrm e}$; $\det\bar{~b}^{\mathrm e} = 1$ then makes
$W\supiso$ independent of $J$ by construction. Both are exact, and
Table~\ref{tab:split} confirms both to machine precision. Three consequences
follow.

\emph{The generic development covers both.} Nothing in
\S\ref{sec:kinematics}--\S\ref{sec:meandilatation}, and nothing in the
discretization of \S\ref{sec:fe-spaces}--\S\ref{sec:local}, refers to either
column of Table~\ref{tab:instances}. The reduction \eqref{eq:collapsed}, the
pointwise relation $\bar p = \kappa\bar\theta$, and the element-local
elimination hold for any $\theta$ satisfying \eqref{eq:thetadef} and any
$W\supiso$ satisfying \eqref{eq:energy}.

\emph{The Stokes limit is instance-independent, and this matters for
\S\ref{sec:results}.} By \eqref{eq:dtheta} and the normalization
\eqref{eq:thetadef}, $\partial\theta/\partial\F = \Id$ at $\F = \Id$ in both
columns, so the pairing that \S\ref{sec:fe-spaces} reduces to the Stokes
pairing is the same for both. The inf-sup analysis, and with it the negative
stability results of \S\ref{sec:beta}, therefore transfer between instances
unchanged: those results are a property of the pair
$(U_h, Q_h)$, not an artifact of having measured the logarithmic one.

\emph{The instances are not interchangeable in plasticity, and this is the
only place they are not.} The last two rows of Table~\ref{tab:instances} are
the entire difference, and they follow from
\eqref{eq:split}: only in the logarithmic instance is the constitutive law
algebraically linear in the strain that the return map sees, which is what lets
a small-strain algorithm be reused without reformulation. \S\ref{sec:plasticity}
develops that instance. Remark~\ref{rem:whichj2} records that it is the other
one that both codes implement.

\subsection{Variations}
\label{sec:variations}

With $\disp \in U$, $\bar\theta, \bar p \in Q$ and test functions
$\testd \in U$ vanishing on $\partial_\disp B$, $\vartheta, \pi \in Q$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:variations}
  \begin{split}
    D_\disp \Pi(\testd) & =
      \int_B \bar p \, \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\disp}[\testd] \diff V
      + \int_B \sdev\!\left(\edev(\disp)\right) : \frac{\partial\edev}{\partial\disp}[\testd] \diff V
      - \int_B R\,\bB\cdot\testd \diff V
      - \int_{\partial_T B} \bT\cdot\testd \diff S = 0,
    \\
    D_{\bar\theta}\Pi(\vartheta) & =
      \int_B \left( \kappa\bar\theta - \bar p \right)\vartheta \diff V = 0,
    \\
    D_{\bar p}\Pi(\pi) & =
      \int_B \left( \theta(\disp) - \bar\theta \right)\pi \diff V = 0 .
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
The kinematic derivatives are
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kinematic}
  \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\disp}[\testd]
    = \theta'(J)\, J\, \F^{-\mathsf{T}} : \Grad\testd,
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial\edev}{\partial\disp}[\testd]
    = \mathbb{L}\supdev(\C) : \sym\!\left(\transpose{\F}\Grad\testd\right),
\end{equation}
with $\mathbb{L}(\C) := \partial \log\C/\partial\C$ the Fr\'echet derivative of
the matrix logarithm \citep{AlMohy.Higham.Relton:2013} and
$\mathbb{L}\supdev := \mathbb{L} - \tfrac13 \Id \otimes \C^{-1}$. The
volumetric derivative follows from Jacobi's formula and \eqref{eq:dtheta}. The
deviatoric expression is written for the Hencky instance of
Table~\ref{tab:instances}; the Simo--Hughes instance replaces it with the
variation of $\bar{~b}^{\mathrm e}$, and nothing outside this equation
changes.

\subsection{Stress reconstruction}
\label{sec:stress}

Collecting \eqref{eq:variations}$_1$ into the form $\int \P : \Grad\testd$
gives, in general,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:P-general}
  \P = \theta'(J)\, J\, \bar p\, \F^{-\mathsf{T}}
     + \F \left( \mathbb{L}\supdev(\C) : \sdev \right).
\end{equation}
Because $\sdev$ here is computed \emph{pointwise} from $\edev(\disp)$ by an
isotropic law, $\sdev$ is coaxial with $\C$ at every quadrature point. The
off-diagonal contributions of $\mathbb{L}\supdev$ in the principal basis then
vanish identically and \eqref{eq:P-general} collapses to the push-forward
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:P-simple}
  \P = \left( \varpi \Id + \sdev \right) \F^{-\mathsf{T}} = \btau \, \F^{-\mathsf{T}},
  \qquad
  \btau := \varpi \Id + \sdev = J\,~\sigma,
  \qquad
  \varpi := \theta'(J)\, J\, \bar p .
\end{equation}

The Kirchhoff pressure $\varpi$ carries the whole of the instance dependence.
For the Hencky instance $\theta'(J)J = 1$ and $\varpi = \bar p$; for the
Simo--Hughes instance $\theta'(J)J = J$ and $\varpi = J\bar p$, giving
$\btau = J\bar p\,\Id + \sdev$ with $\bar p = \kappa(J-1)$, which is the
Kirchhoff stress of \citet[BOX 9.1]{SimoHughes:1998} and is what both codes
compute. That the general form reproduces each implementation without
adjustment is the check that \eqref{eq:thetadef} was normalized correctly.

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:coaxial}
Coaxiality is a consequence of the deviatoric response being pointwise. It
would \emph{not} hold if $\sdev$ were carried as an independent discrete field,
because such a field is only weakly tied to $\C$ and is not coaxial with it at
an arbitrary iterate. In that case \eqref{eq:P-simple} is valid only at the
converged solution and using it to assemble the residual gives an inconsistent
method that appears to work. Equation~\eqref{eq:P-general} is the safe form.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:whynodev}
No deviatoric mixed pair appears because none is needed. Volumetric locking is
a constraint phenomenon: at $\kappa/\mu \gg 1$ the near-isochoric constraint
over-determines the displacement space, and relieving it requires a multiplier.
The deviatoric response is not constrained in the same sense and admits no
analogous locking mode. Adding a deviatoric pair costs degrees of freedom, an
inf-sup condition that has to be established separately, and --- if strain and
stress are placed in different spaces --- a constitutive law evaluated across
two discretizations, which is the aliasing that corrupts plastic strain fields.
\S\ref{sec:fivefield} gives the disciplined way to add strain as an independent
field should a later requirement demand it.
\end{remark}

\section{Discretization}

\subsection{The pair}
\label{sec:fe-spaces}

Let $U_h \subset U$ carry the motion and $Q_h \subset Q$ carry both
$\bar\theta$ and $\bar p$. Two requirements fix the choice.

\emph{Same space for both scalars.} $\bar\theta$ and $\bar p$ are placed in one
space $Q_h$. This is what makes the constraint term vanish identically and the
functional reduce to \eqref{eq:collapsed}; it is the only thing that reduction
needs. Given it, and a quadratic $W\supvol$ (\S\ref{sec:matreq}),
\eqref{eq:variations}$_2$ reads $\bar p = \kappa\bar\theta$ pointwise rather
than in a projected sense, and the composition
$\bar p = \kappa\,\Pi_{Q_h}(\theta)$ is well defined. Splitting the two scalars
across different spaces makes the constitutive relation hold only after a
projection, determines one field only through its projection onto the other's
space, and can leave the discrete system singular.

\emph{Inf-sup stability, in three dimensions.} By \eqref{eq:kinematic} the
pressure--displacement pairing is
$\int \varpi \,\F^{-\mathsf T}\!:\!\Grad\testd$ with
$\varpi = \theta'(J)\,J\,\bar p$, and not $\int \bar p
\,\F^{-\mathsf T}\!:\!\Grad\testd$; the two coincide at $\F = \Id$ and nowhere
else. The normalization \eqref{eq:thetadef} sets $\theta'(1) = 1$, so
$\varpi = \bar p$ there and the pairing reduces to the Stokes pairing
$\int \bar p \Div\testd$ --- in both instances of
Table~\ref{tab:instances}, which is what makes the stability results of
\S\ref{sec:beta} a property of the pair rather than of the material. The
admissible pairs are therefore the inf-sup stable Stokes pairs, and stability
must hold uniformly in $h$ and on the target range of $\kappa/\mu$
\citep{BoffiBrezziFortin:2013}.

The distinction that matters is dimensional and is easy to get wrong:
$P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ is the Crouzeix--Raviart pair in two dimensions
\citep{CrouzeixRaviart:1973} and is \emph{not} inf-sup stable on tetrahedra
without enrichment. The stable three-dimensional choices with a
\emph{discontinuous} pressure require bubble enrichment of the displacement
space. Table~\ref{tab:pairs} lists the candidates.

\begin{table}[h]

Displacement & Pressure & 3D stable & Pressure elimination \\
\midrule
$P_1$                        & $P_0$                   & no  & local \\

\caption{Displacement--pressure pairs on tetrahedra. Local elimination

\label{tab:pairs}
\end{table}

The pair this formulation \emph{requires} is $P_2$ enriched with face and
interior bubbles over $P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$. The two enrichments are different
kinds of object, and an earlier version of this paragraph conflated them. The
interior bubble vanishes on the whole element boundary: it is never shared,
never reached by a boundary condition, and condenses away, adding no global
unknown. A face bubble vanishes on three faces and not on its own, so the two
elements meeting there share it; it is a global unknown and does not condense.
The enrichment therefore does carry a cost in degrees of freedom, which
\S\ref{sec:infsup} quantifies. What survives is the weaker and sufficient
statement: the \emph{pressure} elimination of \S\ref{sec:local} stays
element-by-element, because that rests on $Q_h$ being discontinuous and not on
the displacement space at all.

The pair currently \emph{demonstrated} to work is Taylor--Hood $P_2/P_1$
continuous, whose discrete inf-sup constant is measured settling on $0.2216$ in
\S\ref{sec:beta}, at the cost of a globally coupled pressure that cannot be
eliminated element by element.

Those are not the same statement, and \S\ref{sec:beta} is the reason for
keeping them apart. Both element-local pairs that can be assembled today ---
$P_2/P_0$ and the unenriched $P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ --- were measured
\emph{unstable}, and the enriched pair is unverified rather than verified,
because no element exists to test it on.

\subsection{Local elimination}
\label{sec:local}

Because $Q_h$ is discontinuous, the mass matrix
$[M]_{mn} = \int_B \mu_m \mu_n \diff V$ is block diagonal by element and
constant in the reference configuration. One factorization at setup serves
every Newton iteration of every step, and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:projection}
  M \{\bar\theta\} = \int_B \mu_m \, \theta(\disp) \diff V,
  \qquad
  \bar p = \kappa \bar\theta ,
\end{equation}
is an element-local solve, with $\theta = \log J$ for a Hencky material and
$\theta = J - 1$ for the Simo--Hughes one (\S\ref{sec:matreq}). The second
relation in \eqref{eq:projection} holds \emph{pointwise}, rather than only
after projection, precisely because $W\supvol$ is quadratic; for a material
where it is not, this step becomes a local Newton iteration and the
factorization can no longer be reused across iterations
(Remark~\ref{rem:nonquadratic}). Substituting $\bar p(\disp)$ into
\eqref{eq:variations}$_1$ leaves a displacement-sized nonlinear problem whose
tangent retains the sparsity of the displacement mesh --- the auxiliary fields
never couple two elements. This is the property that makes the composite
tetrahedron affordable, and it is preserved here.

\begin{remark}
The locality is a consequence of the discontinuous pressure, not of the
Hu--Washizu structure. A conforming auxiliary field --- one requiring
inter-element continuity --- would make $\bar p(\disp)$ a global function of
$\disp$, and every tangent--vector product would then require a global solve
with its mass matrix. That is a substantial regression against the incumbent
and is the main reason the pressure is kept discontinuous.
\end{remark}

\section{Plasticity}
\label{sec:plasticity}

This section develops the \emph{logarithmic} instance of
Table~\ref{tab:instances}. It is the only part of the formulation where the
choice of instance changes anything, and the reason to prefer this one; what
follows does not hold verbatim for the Simo--Hughes column, and
Remark~\ref{rem:whichj2} records where that leaves the verification program.

The deviatoric response is evaluated at quadrature points of the displacement
element, on $\edev(\disp)$ computed there. Because the constitutive law is
linear in logarithmic strain, the $J_2$ return map is the small-strain radial
return of \citet{Simo:1992} verbatim:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:return}
  \begin{split}
    \edev^{\mathrm{tr}} &:= \edev(\disp) - \edev^{\mathrm p}_n,
    \qquad
    \sdev^{\mathrm{tr}} := 2\mu\,\edev^{\mathrm{tr}},
    \\
    \Delta\gamma &:= \frac{\norm{\sdev^{\mathrm{tr}}} - \sqrt{2/3}\left[\sigma_y + \mathcal{H}'(\bar\epsilon^{\mathrm p}_n)\right]}{2\mu + \tfrac23 \mathcal{H}''(\bar\epsilon^{\mathrm p}_n)}
      \quad\text{if } f^{\mathrm{tr}} > 0, \quad \text{else } 0,
    \\
    \edev^{\mathrm p}_{n+1} &:= \edev^{\mathrm p}_n + \Delta\gamma\, ~n,
    \qquad ~n := \sdev^{\mathrm{tr}} / \norm{\sdev^{\mathrm{tr}}},
    \\
    \sdev &:= 2\mu\left(\edev(\disp) - \edev^{\mathrm p}_{n+1}\right).
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
Internal variables live at quadrature points and are never projected, so no
interpolation error enters the history. Frame-indifferent interpolation, when
tensorial internal variables must be transferred, follows
\citet{Mota.etal:2013}.

Three properties follow. They are the motivation for the formulation rather
than incidental consequences of it.

\emph{The constitutive law is untouched.} All finite-deformation content is
absorbed into two material-\emph{independent} geometric transforms: the forward
map $\C \mapsto \eps$ and the tangent map $\mathbb{L}(\C)$
\citep{Miehe.Apel.Lambrecht:2002}. Between them sits an unmodified small-strain
algorithm. Any model already written for small strain --- $J_2$, kinematic
hardening, viscoplasticity, damage --- ports without reformulation. This is the
practical content of preferring a Hu--Washizu structure over one built around a
particular stress--strain pairing. Note that this property belongs to the
logarithmic measure and not to the Hu--Washizu structure, so it is the one
place where \S\ref{sec:matreq} does \emph{not} apply: an admissible material
in the sense of \eqref{eq:matsplit} need not have it, and the Simo--Hughes
model both codes ship does not (Remark~\ref{rem:whichj2}).

\emph{Strain and stress never cross discretizations.} $\edev$ is evaluated and
$\sdev$ returned at the same quadrature point of the same element. There is no
projection between the two, so the aliasing that corrupts equivalent plastic
strain in stabilized formulations has no mechanism here.

\emph{No stabilization enters the deviator.} There is no subgrid term, no
penalty and no tuned coefficient anywhere in the formulation, so nothing of the
form \eqref{eq:vms} can reach the return map.

\begin{remark}[The model implemented in both codes, and its bearing on claim~4]
\label{rem:whichj2}
Equation \eqref{eq:return} is the return map this formulation specifies.
Neither Norma nor Carina implements it. Both ship the Simo--Hughes $J_2$ model
\citep{SimoHughes:1998}: a neo-Hookean volumetric--isochoric split,
$W = \tfrac{\kappa}{2}(J-1)^2 + \tfrac{\mu}{2}(\trace\bar{~b}^{\mathrm e} - 3)$,
with the radial return taken on the isochoric elastic left Cauchy--Green tensor
rather than on logarithmic strain. It satisfies \eqref{eq:matsplit} exactly ---
$\det\bar{~b}^{\mathrm e} = 1$ makes the isochoric term independent of $J$ by
construction rather than by an identity --- and its $W\supvol$ is quadratic in
$J-1$, so it is admissible here in the variable $J-1$.

Two consequences follow. The first is that claim~4 of
\S\ref{sec:verify} will be measured on Simo--Hughes unless a logarithmic $J_2$
is written for the purpose; the smoothness of equivalent plastic strain is a
question about whether strain and stress cross discretizations, which is
independent of the elastic split carrying them, so this is a reasonable
substitution --- but it is a substitution.

The second is that the portability argument above is weakened by it. The claim
that any small-strain model ports without reformulation is a property of the
logarithmic measure, and Simo--Hughes does not have it: its return map is
specific to its own structure. The argument stands for the formulation as
specified and does not describe what is currently running.
\end{remark}

\section{Dynamics}

The spatial formulation is unchanged; the mass matrix enters only the
displacement block. Two implementation points deserve care.

\emph{Lumping.} Row-sum lumping of the quadratic tetrahedron produces
non-positive vertex masses and cannot be used. Diagonal-scaling (HRZ) lumping
\citep{HintonRockZienkiewicz:1976}, which scales the diagonal of the consistent
mass to preserve total mass, yields strictly positive entries for $P_2$ and its
bubble enrichment, because the consistent-mass diagonal is positive by
construction. Negative \emph{off-diagonal} entries in the consistent mass are
immaterial; only the lumped result must be positive, and under HRZ it is.

\emph{Critical time step.} Quadratic elements carry a higher maximum
eigenvalue than linear elements on the same mesh, so the explicit stable step
is smaller for a given $h$ --- roughly a factor of two, with the bubble
enrichment reducing it further. Against this, a $P_2$ mesh resolves a given
feature with far fewer elements. Whether the trade-off is favorable
depends on the problem and should be measured.

\section{Optional five-field variant}
\label{sec:fivefield}

Should an independent deviatoric strain field become necessary --- for
non-local regularization, or for internal variables requiring their own
discrete representation --- the systematic extension introduces
$\bar\edev$ and $\bar\sdev$ \emph{in the same discontinuous space}, of the same
degree as $Q_h$. Sharing the space is not merely convenient: it makes the
constitutive relation hold pointwise rather than after a projection, keeps the
return map at shared quadrature points, keeps every elimination element-local,
and leaves the discrete system non-singular. Placing $\bar\edev$ and $\bar\sdev$
in different spaces --- however natural the spaces are individually --- forfeits
all four properties at once. Remark~\ref{rem:coaxial} also applies: with an
independent $\bar\sdev$, the residual must be assembled from
\eqref{eq:P-general} and not from the push-forward \eqref{eq:P-simple}.

\section{Numerical results}
\label{sec:results}

Two experiments have been run. Both work on the linear condensed operator
\eqref{eq:condensed}, so neither needs a solver, a material model, or a mesh
reader; the scripts are in \code{prototype/}, and each reports its own
result.

\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Meshes are generated rather than read, so the refinement sequence is exact.
The unit cube is divided into $N^3$ cubes and each cube into six tetrahedra by
the Freudenthal (Kuhn) subdivision --- the six permutations $(a,b,c)$ giving
$\{~0, ~e_a, ~e_a + ~e_b, (1,1,1)\}$. That
decomposition is conforming across shared cube faces, which the more obvious
five-tetrahedron split is not. Quadratic meshes add edge midpoints in the node
order the reference element expects.

For the stability test the displacement is fixed on the entire boundary and
three operators are assembled,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:infsup-ops}
  K_{ij} = \int \Grad N_i : \Grad N_j \diff V,
  \qquad
  G_{mi} = \int \mu_m \, \Div N_i \diff V,
  \qquad
  M_{mn} = \int \mu_m \mu_n \diff V,
\end{equation}
with $K$ the $H^1$ seminorm --- the standard choice, and necessarily so, since
$~K\supdev$ is singular on exactly the modes at issue. The discrete inf-sup
constant is $\beta_h = \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}$ over the nonzero spectrum of
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:infsup-eig}
  ~G ~K^{-1} \transpose{~G}\, q = \lambda ~M q .
\end{equation}
Boundary data fixed everywhere always leaves one zero mode, the hydrostatic
one, because $\int \Div ~v = 0$ for $~v \in H^1_0$; any zero beyond
that is a spurious pressure mode.

\subsection{Spurious modes on one element}
\label{sec:firstresult}

The soft-mode claim reduces, for linear response, to a rank count on a single
element, and can be settled before any mesh or solver exists. Condensing the
three fields gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:condensed}
  ~K_e = ~K\supdev + \kappa\, \transpose{~G} ~M^{-1} ~G,
  \qquad
  M_{mn} = \int_e \mu_m \mu_n \diff V,
  \qquad
  G_{mi} = \int_e \mu_m \left(\trace ~B\right)_i \diff V .
\end{equation}
$~K\supdev$ annihilates every mode with zero deviatoric strain at quadrature:
the six rigid-body modes, plus the volumetric directions the displacement space
can produce. The second term has rank equal to the number of those directions
the pressure space can see, at most $m$. Anything left over carries neither
deviatoric nor volumetric energy and is \emph{exactly} zero-energy:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:spurious}
  n_{\mathrm{spurious}} = \dim \ker ~K\supdev - 6 - \operatorname{rank} ~K\supvol .
\end{equation}

For the quadratic tetrahedron $\dim\ker ~K\supdev = 10$: six rigid modes and
four volumetric directions, the latter because $\trace\eps(\disp)$ is a $P_1$
field. Table~\ref{tab:census} reports \eqref{eq:spurious} against the measured
spectrum.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
Pressure space & $m$ & $\dim\ker ~K\supdev$ & $\operatorname{rank} ~K\supvol$ & predicted & observed \\
\midrule
$P_0$ constant            & 1  & 10 & 1 & \textbf{3} & \textbf{3} \\
$P_1$ discontinuous       & 4  & 10 & 4 & 0 & 0 \\
$P_2$ discontinuous       & 10 & 10 & 4 & 0 & 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Spurious zero-energy modes of the three-field quadratic tetrahedron at

\label{tab:census}
\end{table}

Three consequences follow.

The constant-pressure element carries three modes that are \emph{exactly}
zero-energy, not merely soft. A mode with no energy at all cannot be tolerated,
which is why a penalty is unavoidable once that pressure space is chosen --- the
remedy in \citet{Foulk.etal:2021} is forced by the space, not by the element.

A $P_1$-discontinuous pressure removes them completely, with no penalty, and
leaves exactly the six rigid-body modes. This is the claim of \S\ref{sec:hw}
confirmed in the setting where it can be verified most directly.

Enriching the pressure further changes nothing: the rank saturates at four,
because four is all the displacement space can produce. Over-enrichment is
wasteful here rather than harmful, at least in this rank sense.

\begin{remark}
This settles the \emph{mechanism} and nothing more. Inf-sup stability is a
global property of a mesh sequence and is invisible on one element; the rank
argument above says which modes exist, not whether the constant in the stability
estimate survives refinement. Claim~1 of \S\ref{sec:verify} remains open, and
with it the question of whether bubble enrichment is needed in practice.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The pair, settled by counting}
\label{sec:infsup}

Section~\ref{sec:firstresult} settled which modes exist on one element. Whether
the constant in the stability estimate survives refinement is a property of a
mesh sequence, and determining it does not require an eigenvalue computation.

Write $n_u$ for the number of free displacement degrees of freedom and $n_p$
for the pressure degrees of freedom. The Schur complement of the mixed system
is $~S = ~G ~K^{-1} \transpose{~G}$, an $n_p \times n_p$ matrix of rank at most
$n_u$. If $n_p > n_u$ then $~S$ is singular \emph{by counting}, the pressure
space carries modes no displacement can resist, and no inf-sup constant exists
--- regardless of the values those constants would otherwise take.

Table~\ref{tab:dofcount} counts them on a Freudenthal-subdivided cube.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Pair & $N$ & $n_u$ & $n_p$ & $n_p/n_u$ \\
\midrule
$P_2/P_0$                              & 12 & 36{,}501 & 10{,}368 & 0.284 \\
$P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$              & 12 & 36{,}501 & 41{,}472 & \textbf{1.136} \\
$P_2 \oplus b_{\mathrm{int}} / P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ & 12 & 67{,}605 & 41{,}472 & 0.613 \\
$P_2 \oplus b_{\mathrm{int}} \oplus b_{\mathrm{face}} / P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$
                                       & 12 & 127{,}221 & 41{,}472 & 0.326 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Degree-of-freedom counts on the unit cube, displacement fixed on the
boundary. Script: \code{prototype/infsup.jl}.}
\label{tab:dofcount}
\end{table}

The middle row is the result, and the asymptotics behind it are exact rather
than suggestive. The quadratic nodes of this mesh family fill the $(2N+1)^3$
grid, so with the whole boundary fixed $n_u = 3(2N-1)^3 \to 24N^3$, while
$n_p = 4 \cdot 6N^3 = 24N^3$ regardless of boundary data. The ratio tends to
one. \emph{The pair is critically determined in the limit}, which is precisely
the situation a uniform inf-sup condition cannot survive.

\begin{remark}[What the finite-$N$ column does and does not show]
\label{rem:bc-caveat}
The ratios above exceed one at every $N$ in the table, but that part is an
artifact of the boundary condition and should not be read as the result.
Fixing the whole boundary removes $O(N^2)$ displacement unknowns and none of
the pressure, so the approach to the limit is from above; fixing only one face
gives $n_u = 6N(2N+1)^2$ and the approach is from below, with the ratio
\emph{under} one at every computable $N$. Both tend to one, and it is the limit
that carries the argument. The finite-$N$ sign is a property of how the block
is held.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}
$P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ on tetrahedra is not inf-sup stable, and no
refinement rescues it.
\end{proposition}

Adding one interior bubble per element supplies $3n_{\mathrm{el}}$ further
displacement degrees of freedom and moves the ratio to $0.613$; adding the four
face bubbles as well --- the enrichment \S\ref{sec:fe-spaces} actually calls
for --- supplies $3n_{\mathrm{f}}$ more, one vector unknown per interior face,
and moves it to $0.326$. Both clear the bound. This is the quantitative content
of the recommendation in \S\ref{sec:fe-spaces}: the enrichment is not a matter
of preference; it is what makes the count admissible. It also fixes its price,
since the face bubbles are global: the enriched space is $3.5$ times the
displacement dimension of $P_2/P_0$ on the same mesh.

\noindent
Whether that limiting degeneracy is the practically decisive objection is a
separate question, and \S\ref{sec:assembled} answers it differently than
expected.

\subsection{Assembled behavior, and a correction}
\label{sec:assembled}

The element census of \S\ref{sec:firstresult} is a local statement. An
element-local mode survives assembly only if its trace is compatible with every
neighbor across every shared face, so the global count can be far smaller than
$3n_{\mathrm{el}}$, and whether it is bears directly on the argument against a
constant pressure, which rests on that count.

Table~\ref{tab:assembled} assembles $~K\supdev + \kappa \transpose{~G} ~M^{-1}
~G$ over meshes under two boundary conditions: the whole boundary fixed, and
only $z = 0$ fixed. The second matters because it is far closer to the
confined-but-not-clamped conditions under which soft modes are reported.

The locking measure is again a rank. A displacement mode costs volumetric
energy exactly when it lies outside $\ker ~G$, so as $\kappa \to \infty$ the
reachable deformations collapse onto that kernel and
$\dim\ker ~G = n_u - \operatorname{rank} ~G$ is the discretely isochoric
subspace. A large fraction means the element still deforms; a collapse toward
zero is volumetric locking.

The fraction is read against a bound rather than against intuition. Since
$\operatorname{rank}~G \le n_p$, it cannot fall below $1 - n_p/n_u$. A pair
that \emph{attains} that bound spends every pressure unknown on a distinct
displacement direction and wastes none; one well above it is leaving pressure
modes unresisted; one that cannot reach it, because $n_p > n_u$, is

Three findings, and the first and third correct this note.

\paragraph{The constant-pressure soft modes do not survive assembly.}
Zero spurious modes, under both boundary conditions, at every mesh. Nor are
they present as merely low-energy ones: the smallest eigenvalue relative to the
deviatoric scale is $1.51\times10^{-3}$ for $P_0$ against $1.84\times10^{-3}$
for $P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ on the same mesh --- indistinguishable, and both
reflecting the long-wavelength modes of a lightly constrained block. The three
modes per element of \S\ref{sec:firstresult} are real but element-local, and
their traces do not match across faces.

So the soft-mode objection to a constant pressure, on \emph{these} elements, is
not supported at the assembled level, and the earlier framing of it here
overstated the case. The composite tetrahedron of \citet{Foulk.etal:2021} is
built on a subdivided-linear displacement space rather than $P_2$, which is a
different kernel and is not modeled here; that lineage's soft mode may well be
specific to it. Nothing above contradicts their observation; it does not
reproduce it on a quadratic tetrahedron.

\paragraph{The unenriched pair locks, and that is the real objection.}
With the boundary fixed, $P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ retains 3--7\% of its
deformation modes as $\kappa$ grows, against 58--63\% for $P_0$. Under lighter
support it is 37--41\% against 80\%. Either way it is the pair that
over-constrains, and locking is a far more damaging failure in practice than a
spurious mode, because it is silent: the element returns a stiff response.

\paragraph{The enrichment, assembled rather than extrapolated.} An earlier
version of this table did not build the bubble. It added $3n_{\mathrm{el}}$ to
$n_u$ and carried $\operatorname{rank}~G$ over from the unenriched pair, on the
reasoning that the interior bubble vanishes on the element boundary, and
reported an isochoric fraction of 0.56--0.62. The reasoning is sound for $P_0$,
where the bubble columns of $~G$ are identically zero
(Remark~\ref{rem:whyface}), and wrong for $P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$, where they are
not: the bubble raises $\operatorname{rank}~G$ from 957 to 1532 at $N = 4$, and
the isochoric fraction is $0.252$--$0.298$, not $0.56$--$0.62$. The
extrapolation was out by a factor of two, and in the optimistic direction.

The full enrichment does reach 0.61--0.63, in line with $P_0$ and without
$P_0$'s element-level degeneracy --- so the discarded figure was numerically
close to the right answer for a pair it was not describing. Both enriched pairs
attain the bound $1 - n_p/n_u$ to three digits, meaning no pressure unknown is
spent twice; and for the full pair
$n_p - \operatorname{rank}~G = 1$ at every mesh, the hydrostatic mode alone.
That, rather than the soft-mode argument, is what the enrichment is for.

\subsection{Stability, measured against a validated control}
\label{sec:beta}

The counting argument of \S\ref{sec:infsup} is \emph{necessary}. Whether it is
sufficient is a different question, and the answer is no. Establishing this
carefully matters, because an earlier version of this note relied on the count.

Two changes make a long mesh sequence reachable. With $~K = \transpose{~P} ~L
\transpose{~L} ~P$ from a sparse Cholesky, $\transpose{q} ~G ~K^{-1}
\transpose{~G} q = \lVert ~L^{-1} (\transpose{~G} q)_{[p]} \rVert^2$, so
$\beta_h$ is the smallest \emph{nonzero singular value} of $~W = ~L^{-1}
\transpose{~G} ~M^{-1/2}$ --- never forming $~G ~K^{-1} \transpose{~G}$, and so
never squaring the condition number of the very quantity being measured. And
the null dimension is fixed independently as $n_p - \operatorname{rank}~G$, so
the cut is taken by index rather than by selecting a threshold. In practice the
gap is unambiguous: $O(10^{-15})$ below it against $O(10^{-2})$ above.

The sweep also carries a \emph{positive} control. A negative control
alone --- $P_1/P_0$, known unstable --- cannot detect a test that reports every
pair as unstable.

The control behaves exactly as it must: $\beta_h$ rises and settles on
$0.2216$, flat to three digits over the last three refinements. Taylor--Hood is
stable in three dimensions and the test reports it as such, which is what
validates reading the remaining rows.

\paragraph{Neither unenriched element-local pair is stable.} $P_2/P_0$ decays
at a rate approaching one, despite passing the degree-of-freedom count at
$0.284$; $P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ decays faster still.

\paragraph{The interior bubble alone does not rescue the pair.} It raises
$\beta_h$ by about half at every mesh, and the decay rate falls from $1.05$ to
$0.77$, but the sequence still decays. This is the row the count could not have
predicted: it clears the count at $0.613$ (Table~\ref{tab:dofcount}) and fails
the test.

\paragraph{The full Crouzeix--Raviart pair is stable, and this is the first
affirmative result here.} With the face bubbles as well, $\beta_h$ rises
monotonically to $0.2968$ and is flat to three digits over the last three
refinements, at a fitted rate of $-0.011$ --- the control's own signature, and
at a \emph{higher} constant than the control's $0.2216$. The pressure null
space is exactly one dimensional at every mesh in the sequence, the hydrostatic
mode and nothing else, so there are no spurious pressure modes to speak of
either. \emph{The pair \S\ref{sec:fe-spaces} calls for works, and the reduced
enrichment does not.}

\begin{remark}[What separates the two enrichments]
\label{rem:whyface}
The interior bubble cannot act on a constant pressure at all: it vanishes on
the whole element boundary, so $\int_{B_e}\!\Div ~b_{\mathrm{int}} = 0$ and its
columns of $~G$ against a $P_0$ mode are identically zero. It reaches only the
\emph{linear} pressure modes, which is why it changes
$P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ and leaves $P_2/P_0$ alone --- the last row of
Table~\ref{tab:beta} reproduces $P_2/P_0$ to four digits, as it must, and is
carried as a check that the enrichment is wired into $~G$ correctly rather than
merely into $~K$. The face bubbles are what supply a displacement response to
the pressure jump across a face, which is the mode a discontinuous pressure
actually needs resisted.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The count is necessary, not sufficient]
\label{rem:necessary}
$P_2/P_0$ and $P_2 \oplus b_{\mathrm{int}}/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ are both
counterexamples to any reading of \S\ref{sec:infsup} as having settled the
question. Each passes the count with margin --- at $0.284$ and $0.613$ --- and
each fails the test. A ratio above one \emph{refutes} a pair; a ratio below one
establishes nothing, and the two pairs that clear the count by the widest
margin are not the one that turns out to work.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Mesh dependence]
\label{rem:structured}
These results are for the Freudenthal subdivision, a highly structured family,
and structured meshes are where pair counterexamples characteristically arise
--- the $P_1/P_0$ checkerboard is at its worst on them. A pair can degenerate
on a structured mesh and remain well behaved on a general one. The accurate
statement is that $P_2/P_0$ and $P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ decay \emph{here};
confirming that
as a property of the pairs rather than of the mesh needs a sequence of
unstructured meshes, which this study does not have.
\end{remark}

The zero counts for $P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ --- 111, 285, 579, 1029 --- are
not an independent finding: they are $n_p - n_u$ to within a few modes, which

supported by these data. A dense generalized eigensolver caps the
sequence at $N = 5$, far short of an asymptotic regime, and $\beta_h \sim
h^{1/2}$ over less than a decade of $h$ is equally consistent with a
pre-asymptotic transient. Establishing it would need a sparse eigensolver and
meshes several refinements finer.

\begin{remark}
The contrast between the two tests is the methodological point. The counting
argument is exact, needs no solver, and scales to any mesh size; the spectral
one is approximate, expensive, and here mostly re-derives what counting already
gave. Where a necessary condition settles the question, it should be checked
before the eigenvalue computation is attempted.
\end{remark}

\section{What must be verified}
\label{sec:verify}

The formulation rests on falsifiable claims. They are stated explicitly so
that the first round of numerical work is decisive.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The pair is inf-sup stable in three dimensions, uniformly on
        $\kappa/\mu \le 10^4$.} \emph{Settled affirmatively for the
        Crouzeix--Raviart pair, negatively for every other element-local pair}
        (\S\ref{sec:beta}). On the mesh family tested $\beta_h$ decays for
        $P_2/P_0$, for $P_2/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$, and for
        $P_2 \oplus b_{\mathrm{int}}/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$, while
        $P_2 \oplus b_{\mathrm{int}} \oplus b_{\mathrm{face}}/P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$
        settles on $0.2968$ at a fitted rate of $-0.011$, above the
        Taylor--Hood control's $0.2216$ and with a one-dimensional pressure
        null space at every mesh. The face bubbles are the part that matters;
        the interior bubble alone is not enough
        (Remark~\ref{rem:whyface}). What remains is an unstructured mesh
        sequence (Remark~\ref{rem:structured}) and confirmation at finite
        $\kappa/\mu$, both operators here being the incompressible limit.
  \item \textbf{No soft modes.} \emph{Settled, and not as expected.} Three
        spurious zero-energy modes per element for a constant pressure
        (\S\ref{sec:firstresult}), but none of them survive assembly under
        either boundary condition tested (\S\ref{sec:assembled}). The
        soft-mode argument against a constant pressure does not hold on
        quadratic tetrahedra; the argument for the enriched pair rests on
        locking instead.
  \item \textbf{No volumetric locking.} \emph{Settled for every pair}
        (\S\ref{sec:assembled}), by measuring the isochoric subspace
        $\dim\ker ~G$ rather than by running benchmarks:
        $P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ locks badly, $P_0$ does not, and the fully
        enriched pair reaches $0.61$--$0.63$ while attaining the count bound
        $1 - n_p/n_u$. What remains is a benchmark confirmation, since a
        healthy isochoric fraction is necessary and not obviously sufficient.
  \item \textbf{Plastic strain fields are smooth.} The direct comparison
        against stabilized formulations. Equivalent plastic strain in a
        confined plastic zone should show no oscillation and require no
        filtering. This will be measured on the Simo--Hughes $J_2$ model both
        codes ship rather than on the logarithmic return map of
        \eqref{eq:return}, for the reasons in Remark~\ref{rem:whichj2}.
  \item \textbf{Element-local cost.} The assembled tangent must retain
        displacement-mesh sparsity, confirming the elimination never couples
        elements.
\end{enumerate}

The benchmark set is the one on which the composite tetrahedron is known to
struggle: punch indentation, a nearly-incompressible Cook's membrane in three
dimensions, large-rotation twist of a bar, and a weak shock in a high-$\kappa/\mu$
material. The comparison of record is against the composite tetrahedron on the
same meshes.

\section{Risks}

\emph{The pair is settled; its cost is now the risk.} The central risk of the
previous version --- that no element-local pair had been shown to work --- is
retired: $P_2 \oplus b_{\mathrm{int}} \oplus b_{\mathrm{face}}$ over
$P_1^{\mathrm{disc}}$ is stable, with a constant above Taylor--Hood's
(\S\ref{sec:beta}). What that verification also established is the price. The
face bubbles are shared between neighbors, so they are global unknowns and do
not condense; at $N = 12$ the enriched space carries $127{,}221$ displacement
degrees of freedom against $36{,}501$ for $P_2/P_0$ on the same mesh, a factor
of $3.5$. Locality survives where the note claimed it --- the \emph{pressure}
elimination is still element-by-element, since that depends only on $Q_h$ being
discontinuous --- but the claim that the enrichment adds no global unknowns was
false, and \S\ref{sec:fe-spaces} has been corrected.

Whether that factor of $3.5$ is repaid by the accuracy of a stable pair on
coarser meshes is not established, and it is the comparison that now decides
whether the formulation is worth preferring to Taylor--Hood, whose pressure is
global but whose displacement space is a third the size. That is a benchmark
question, not an operator question, and it is answered on the benchmark set
below rather than by any of the counts above.

\emph{Quadratic tetrahedra are more expensive per element.} The claim that
fewer of them are needed is plausible and unproven for this problem class. It
should be measured on the benchmark set, not assumed.

\emph{The explicit regime is the weakest part.} HRZ lumping resolves
positivity, but the reduced critical time step is real. If weak shocks can be
run with the implicit dynamic integrator the objection disappears; if they must
be explicit, this trade needs measuring before commitment.

\emph{The formulation restricts the material, and the restriction is narrow.}
\S\ref{sec:matreq} requires an exact volumetric--isochoric split with a
quadratic volumetric energy, and of the seven materials Norma implements only
two qualify. That is narrower than the earlier framing of this note implied,
and it weakens the claim of material-agnosticism that motivated the choice of
a Hu--Washizu structure. Two mitigations exist, and neither is without cost:
admitting the three split-but-non-quadratic materials costs a local
Newton per element (Remark~\ref{rem:nonquadratic}), and anisotropic or
non-separable models are outside the split altogether and would need the
five-field variant of \S\ref{sec:fivefield} or a different treatment
entirely. The
restriction should be stated whenever the formulation is compared against
$\bar F$, which carries no such requirement.

\emph{Nothing here is novel in its parts.} Mixed volumetric treatment
\citep{SimoTaylorPister:1985}, logarithmic-strain plasticity
\citep{Miehe.Apel.Lambrecht:2002} and stable Stokes pairs
\citep{BoffiBrezziFortin:2013} are all established. The contribution, if there
is one, is the claim that combining them correctly removes the need for the
stabilization that both the composite-tetrahedron and variational-multiscale
lineages carry --- and that claim carries only the weight of the numerical
evidence supporting it. Face-hybridized methods
\citep{AbbasErnPignet:2019} remain the alternative to weigh if it fails.
